Orientation

The systems to know

Five names come up constantly. Learn what each one is before anything else, because every term later in this book lives inside one of them.

Sky Systemz / SkyOS
the company & its platform

Sky Systemz is the fintech (a DBA of ODP Solutions) that optimizes payments for physical, field-based industries — construction, concrete, aggregates, supply, distribution. SkyOS is its product: the web-based operating system a merchant logs into for selling, customers, payments, inventory, and performance.

SkyFi / Pay by Bank
pay-by-bank & embedded finance

The banking side — business checking and ACH "Pay by Bank," built into SkyOS. It moves payments off cards and onto bank rails to kill fees. This is the cross-sell.

Command Alkon / Command Cloud
the industry ops platform

The third-party platform ready-mix and aggregates operators already dispatch in. Our payments embed directly inside it, so the operator collects without leaving the software they run their day in.

Fractal / EasyPay
the payments product line

Fractal is the gateway and processing infrastructure; EasyPay is its product line — the payments product embedded into Command Cloud. EasyPay is a Fractal product, not a Command Alkon one.

Outpave
expense management & card facilitation

An all-in-one financial platform for construction and trades: corporate and virtual cards, real-time expense tracking, and branded invoicing that collects payment — with sync to QuickBooks, Xero, and Sage. It lets a business run card facilitation and invoicing from its own system. More at outpave.com.

Part one

Sales 101 — the language you'll use

Everything here happens at the front or the back of the deal: the money you help a merchant collect, what it costs them, the products that ease their pain, and the pipeline you move them through. If you can explain these in plain English to a lumber-yard owner, you can sell.

How a payment actually moves
Authorization

DefinitionThe instant check that confirms a customer's card or account has the funds before a sale is approved.

Why the merchant caresIt's the "yes" that lets them hand over product with confidence the money is good.

For exampleA customer taps a card at the scale house; the payment is authorized in a second before the truck is released.

Batch or batching

DefinitionAll the day's approved transactions grouped into one submission for settlement, usually at a set cutoff time.

Why the merchant caresThe batch cutoff decides whether today's sales fund tomorrow or the day after — a clean answer to "when do I get paid?"

For exampleApproved sales are grouped into an evening batch to line them up for next-day deposit.

Capture

DefinitionThe step where an authorized payment is locked in so the funds will actually transfer.

Why the merchant caresAuthorization alone doesn't pay them — capture is what turns "approved" into "collected."

For exampleThe payment is captured automatically once the invoice is finalized.

Disbursement or funding

DefinitionThe payout of settled funds into the merchant's business checking account.

Why the merchant caresIt's the finish line — money in their account, ready to make payroll and buy materials.

For exampleAfter settlement, funds land in the merchant's linked business checking account.

Settlement

DefinitionThe bank-to-bank hand-off that finalizes a payment and moves money toward the merchant's account.

Why the merchant caresThis is the moment the sale becomes real cash on its way to their bank.

For exampleOnce the batch closes, the transactions settle so funds are ready to disburse.

What the merchant pays
Basis points (BPS)

DefinitionThe unit for talking about rates precisely: 1 basis point = 0.01%, so 100 bps = 1%.

Why a rep caresIt's how pricing conversations happen. "25 bps" sounds tiny but across millions in volume it's real money — you need to speak it fluently.

For exampleThe gap between 250 and 275 bps is 0.25% — small per sale, large across a year of processing.

Interchange fee

DefinitionThe fee set by Visa and Mastercard that the merchant's bank pays to the customer's bank on every card transaction.

Why the merchant caresIt's the single biggest slice of what card acceptance costs them — the thing they feel and complain about.

For exampleEvery card sale carries interchange; it's usually the largest line in a merchant's processing cost.

Interchange Plus (IC+)

DefinitionA transparent pricing model: the merchant pays the true interchange rate plus a fixed, visible markup — not a blended flat rate that hides the margin.

Why the merchant caresThey can finally see what's cost versus what's the processor's cut. Transparency is a selling point against flat-rate competitors.

For exampleOn an IC+ statement, interchange is separated cleanly from the processor's margin.

Network fee

DefinitionA small fee the card networks charge for using their infrastructure, on top of interchange.

Why the merchant caresIt's a fixed cost of accepting cards they can't negotiate away — context for why the total rate looks the way it does.

For exampleA network fee, usually under 0.15%, rides along with every Visa or Mastercard transaction.

Processing fee

DefinitionThe all-in cost per transaction — interchange, network fees, and the processor's markup combined.

Why the merchant caresThis is the number they actually compare between providers. Knowing what's inside it lets you show where Sky saves them money.

For exampleA merchant pays one blended processing fee per sale.

Service fee or surcharge

DefinitionAn added fee passed to the customer who chooses to pay by credit card, offsetting the merchant's processing cost.

Why the merchant caresIt can move card fees off their P&L entirely — but the rules are strict, which is exactly why they need a provider who handles it compliantly.

For exampleA merchant applies a 3% service fee when a customer opts to pay by credit card.

The products you sell
3DS (3-D Secure)in SkyOS + EasyPay

DefinitionA verification step that confirms the person entering a card online is the real cardholder before the payment clears.

Why the merchant caresIt shuts down fraud and chargebacks on card-not-present payments — the riskiest kind, because the card was never in hand.

For exampleA merchant sends a SkyOS payment link; the customer gets a one-time prompt from their bank before the charge goes through.

Dual routing or dual processingin SkyOS / Fractal · verify

DefinitionGiving the system two processing paths and letting it automatically pick the cheaper one per transaction. It's an addition to the merchant's setup, not a replacement.

Why the merchant caresLower cost on every transaction with nothing new to manage — the system does the choosing.

For exampleDual routing quietly selects the lower-cost path on each sale without the merchant lifting a finger.

Hosted payment pagein SkyOS · verify

DefinitionA secure page where a customer enters card details, so sensitive data never touches the merchant's own systems.

Why the merchant caresIt takes them out of the security firing line — less compliance burden, less risk.

For exampleA merchant sends customers to a hosted page to collect payment safely.

NFC or tap-to-pay

DefinitionContactless payment where a card or phone is tapped near a terminal instead of swiped or inserted.

Why the merchant caresFaster checkout at the counter or the truck — less waiting, especially for COD pickups.

For exampleThe PAX A920 terminal takes a tap-to-pay from a customer's phone at will-call.

Pay by Bankin SkyFi

DefinitionAn ACH option that lets a customer pay an invoice straight from their bank account — no card involved.

Why the merchant caresIt kills card fees on those payments, speeds up deposits, and simplifies reconciliation. This is the flagship cost-saver.

For exampleA merchant offers Pay by Bank to account customers who'd otherwise pay by card, cutting the fee to a fraction.

Payment optimizationin SkyOS

DefinitionSteering customers toward the lowest-cost way to pay by showing the price difference right at checkout.

Why the merchant caresCustomers shift their own behavior when they can see card costs more — the merchant saves without policing anyone.

For exampleWhen Pay by Bank shows as cheaper than card at checkout, more customers pick it on their own.

Same-day depositin SkyFi

DefinitionA funding option for underwritten SkyFi merchants that delivers Pay by Bank payments the same business day.

Why the merchant caresIt removes the "but ACH is slow" objection — they get paid as fast as a card, without card fees.

For exampleAn underwritten account gets Pay by Bank funds same-day, matching card speed at a cost of pennies.

Tokenizationin Fractal

DefinitionReplacing a stored card number with a random stand-in "token," so real account data is never sitting exposed.

Why the merchant caresStored cards for repeat customers stay usable but not stealable — safety without friction.

For exampleA saved card is tokenized so the actual number is never held in the clear.

The problems you solve
AR aging or DSO

Definition"Days sales outstanding" is how long it takes to collect after a sale; "aging" is how overdue the unpaid invoices are.

Why the merchant caresSlow collection is a tax on their cash — money already spent on labor and materials, stuck in someone else's hands. Faster payment is the pitch.

For exampleIf account customers take 52 days on average to pay, DSO is 52; invoices past 90 days are "aged" and a cash-flow warning.

Chargeback

DefinitionA customer-initiated dispute that reverses a completed card payment — the merchant loses the funds and pays a penalty fee.

Why the merchant caresA single reversal can wipe out the margin on several loads. Reducing them is a concrete dollar value you can sell.

For exampleA customer disputes a delivery charge; the supplier has the money pulled back plus a fee, even though the product shipped.

Reconciliation (ticket-to-cash)

DefinitionMatching each payment back to the right invoice, ticket, and job so the books are accurate.

Why the merchant caresWhen payments live in one system and tickets in another, this is manual, error-prone misery. Doing it automatically saves real office hours.

For exampleA supplier ties a card payment back to the specific delivery ticket and job by hand every month — until it's automated.

Refund

DefinitionReturning funds to a customer after a completed sale.

Why the merchant caresKnowing a refund reverses a settled sale (versus a void, which cancels one before it settles) keeps them from double-charging or panicking.

For exampleA customer overpays; the merchant issues a refund directly in SkyOS.

Void

DefinitionCancelling a transaction before it settles, so no money ever moves.

Why the merchant caresCatching a mistake before batch close means no refund fee and no waiting — a clean cancel.

For exampleA customer switches payment methods before the day's batch closes; the merchant voids the first charge.

The deal paperwork & IDs you'll name
GPV (Gross Processing Volume)

DefinitionThe total dollar amount a merchant runs through processing over a period.

Why a rep caresIt's the size of the account. It drives residuals, and it determines which underwriting path a deal takes.

For exampleA merchant processing $400K a month has roughly $4.8M in annual GPV.

MCC (Merchant Category Code)

DefinitionA four-digit code the card networks use to classify what kind of business a merchant is.

Why a rep caresThe MCC shapes a merchant's interchange rates — so it quietly affects their costs and how they're underwritten.

For exampleA ready-mix supplier is coded MCC 1771 (concrete work).

MID (Merchant ID)

DefinitionThe unique number assigned to a merchant's processing account, used for tracking and settlement.

Why a rep caresIt's how every transaction and residual ties back to that merchant — the account's fingerprint.

For exampleEvery SkyOS transaction is tied to a MID for accurate reporting.

MPA (Merchant Processing Agreement)

DefinitionThe contract that sets the terms between the merchant and the payment provider; it must be signed before live processing.

Why a rep caresIt's the gate into registration — no MPA, no processing. Knowing it's coming lets you set expectations early.

For exampleA merchant signs the MPA, and the deal is ready to move into registration.

Part one · the pipeline

The deal stages, start to close

First: a deal (also called an opportunity) is a single potential sale, tracked from first interest all the way to close. In HubSpot, each merchant you're working is one deal that moves down these stages as it progresses. This is a pipeline, not a timeline — a deal doesn't sit on a clock; it moves when the work moves it.

1

Interest Verified

The deal is created here. You've confirmed the merchant is genuinely interested — a real conversation happened and there's a reason to work the opportunity. Not just curiosity.

For exampleA lumber-yard owner replies to your outreach, frustrated with card fees. You create the deal; it lands in Interest Verified.

2

Qualified to Buy

The merchant has surfaced real pain, shown buying intent, and you can see our solution fits. Interested became a fit.

For exampleThe owner says fees are eating margin and they run ~$400K a month. Pain, intent, and fit — move to Qualified to Buy.

3

Demo Scheduled

A demo is officially booked on a sales engineer's calendar. By this point pain points are known, business qualification has happened, and the selling points are identified — the demo isn't a fishing trip.

For exampleThe SE's calendar holds a Thursday 2pm demo; the stage moves to Demo Scheduled.

4

Demo Complete

The sales engineer has delivered the demo. There's no qualified-or-unqualified split at this stage — if a demo happened, the deal lands here.

For exampleThe SE finishes the walkthrough; the deal moves to Demo Complete regardless of how it went.

5

Decision Maker Bought In

The person who actually makes the decision has given a verbal yes or a go-ahead. You're moving forward with the opportunity.

For exampleThe owner says "let's do it." The deal moves to Decision Maker Bought In.

6

Registration Submitted

The merchant has completed all registration documentation and it's been submitted to the underwriting team for review and approval.

For exampleSigned MPA and supporting docs go to Underwriting; the deal moves to Registration Submitted.

7

Registered

Our internal underwriting team has verified the registration. It's processed, approved, and good to go.

For exampleUnderwriting clears the account; the deal moves to Registered.

8

Training Stage

Training has been scheduled with the merchant to get them ready to process.

For exampleThe onboarding session is booked; the deal moves to Training Stage.

✓

Closed Won

The merchant has successfully processed payments with us and met the payment threshold. They're a live customer.

For exampleThe first live batch clears; the deal is Closed Won.

✕

Closed Lost

The opportunity didn't move forward — unqualified on business or technical grounds, or we simply fell short. Often this happens after Demo Complete when business qualification didn't happen up front; it also catches any opportunity we lose, demo or not.

For exampleAfter the demo, technical qualification reveals the merchant can't integrate the way they'd need to.

↩

Closed Lost — Churned

They were a live customer but have since left, for whatever reason.

For exampleAn account that had been processing closes six months in.

One stage still needs a definitionQuick Registration exists in the pipeline but doesn't have a confirmed definition yet — it's flagged here rather than guessed at. Once you define it, this entry gets written to match the others.

Part one · speaking their build language

The solutioning timeline

A different timeline from the pipeline above. The pipeline is our deal moving through HubSpot. This is the merchant's project lifecycle — especially a contractor's — from idea to go-live. You don't run any of it; you just need to recognize the words so you can speak to a go-live at a high level and never over-promise the middle.

first
Planning
Scoping what's being built and what it needs to do. Requirements, not code.
then
Prototype
A rough, throwaway version to test whether the idea holds up.
then
Design
Deciding how it will look and work — the blueprint the build follows.
then
Development
The real building. Turning the design into a working system.
then
Testing
Checking it works and is safe before anyone relies on it.
finally
Live
In production — real users, real money, real work running through it.

When a merchant says "we're still in testing" or "once we go live," you know exactly where they are and can meet them there — without promising a date the implementation team owns.

Part two

Behind the curtain — understand, don't run

This is the middle of the process: underwriting, banking, compliance, and the processing backbone. A rep never operates any of it — but you must understand it, because the fastest way to lose a merchant's trust (and drive your own turnover) is to over-promise something you don't control. Each back-office term names the human who owns it, so you route instead of guess.

Underwriting & registration
$3M annual threshold

DefinitionWhen a merchant's annual processing volume crosses roughly $3M, the deal takes a heavier underwriting path that requires a second concurrence — a second internal sign-off.

Why a rep should careYour biggest, most exciting accounts move slower through registration by design. Knowing this keeps you from promising a large merchant a fast turn.

For exampleA high-volume aggregates supplier trips the $3M threshold, so registration needs the second-concurrence review.

Who to callJess, in Underwriting.

KYB (Know Your Business)

DefinitionVerifying that a business is legitimate — confirming its registration and ownership structure.

Why a rep should careIf a merchant's business docs are messy, this is where a deal slows. Setting the expectation early saves a painful surprise at registration.

For exampleUnderwriting confirms a company's registration details and who actually owns it.

Who to callJess, in Underwriting.

KYC (Know Your Customer)

DefinitionVerifying the identity of the individuals behind an account before granting financial access.

Why a rep should careIt's a legal requirement, not red tape. Framing it that way turns a "why do you need my ID?" moment into a sign of a serious provider.

For exampleA merchant submits ID and proof of address so the account can complete KYC before activation.

Who to callJess, in Underwriting.

OFAC screening

DefinitionAn automated check of every applicant against the U.S. Treasury's sanctions list, run during SkyFi onboarding.

Why a rep should careIt's one more routine reason activation takes a beat. You can reassure without inventing a timeline.

For exampleA new applicant is auto-screened against the OFAC list before the account is approved.

Who to callJess, in Underwriting.

TIN & the 147-C letter

DefinitionThe Taxpayer Identification Number identifies the business to the IRS. A 147-C is the IRS letter that re-confirms that number when records don't match.

Why a rep should careA TIN mismatch is one of the most common reasons registration stalls. Knowing the 147-C exists lets you tell a stuck merchant exactly what unblocks them.

For exampleUnderwriting flags a name/TIN mismatch; the merchant requests a 147-C from the IRS to clear it.

Who to callJess, in Underwriting.

Underwriting

DefinitionThe review of a business's risk profile — credit, ownership, compliance — before payment processing is switched on.

Why a rep should careThis is why boarding isn't instant. You can reassure a merchant the review is routine; you cannot promise an approval or a timeline you don't control.

For exampleA merchant's application sits in the underwriting queue for risk review before activation.

Who to callJess, in Underwriting.

Compliance & risk
BSA / AML

DefinitionThe Bank Secrecy Act and Anti-Money-Laundering rules requiring financial providers to monitor for and report suspicious activity.

Why a rep should careIt's the reason certain accounts and patterns get extra scrutiny — not the provider being difficult, but the law doing its job.

For exampleMonitoring flags unusual transfer patterns to satisfy BSA/AML obligations.

CTR (Currency Transaction Report)

DefinitionA required government filing for cash or cash-equivalent transactions over $10,000 in a single business day.

Why a rep should careContext for cash-heavy merchants — big cash days trigger routine reporting, nothing sinister.

For exampleGrasshopper files a CTR automatically when a large merchant deposit crosses the threshold.

PCI DSS

DefinitionThe card-industry security standard for safely handling cardholder data across processing, storage, and transmission.

Why a rep should careA merchant may ask "is my customers' card data safe?" The answer is yes, and PCI DSS Level 1 is the name for it.

For exampleFractal maintains PCI DSS Level 1 compliance so merchants meet card-security requirements.

SAR (Suspicious Activity Report)

DefinitionA confidential filing a financial institution makes when it spots transactions that may signal fraud or money laundering.

Why a rep should careYou'll never file one, but knowing it exists explains why compliance sometimes can't share details about an account.

For exampleSkyFi files SARs through Grasshopper when activity meets reporting thresholds.

SOC 2

DefinitionAn audit framework validating how a company secures and manages customer data.

Why a rep should careIt's trust in a certificate — useful proof for a larger, more cautious merchant that Sky is buttoned-up.

For exampleSky undergoes annual SOC 2 Type 2 certification.

Transaction monitoring & risk scoring

DefinitionContinuous, automated watching of payments for unusual patterns, and a numeric risk score that guides how closely a merchant is overseen.

Why a rep should careIt explains why a sudden spike in refunds or volume can trigger a review — the system is protecting the merchant and Sky alike.

For exampleA sudden surge in chargebacks raises a merchant's risk score and prompts a compliance look.

The processing backbone
ACH (Automated Clearing House)

DefinitionThe U.S. network that moves bank-to-bank transfers in batches, typically within one to two business days, far cheaper than card.

Why a rep should careIt powers Pay by Bank. The cost savings you sell come directly from moving a payment off cards and onto ACH.

For exampleA Pay by Bank invoice settles over ACH rather than Visa or Mastercard.

Clearing

DefinitionThe step where transaction details pass from the merchant's (acquiring) bank to the customer's (issuing) bank before settlement.

Why a rep should careIt's the invisible middle between "approved" and "funded" — helpful when a merchant asks why money isn't instant.

For exampleClearing happens between banks after authorization and before settlement finalizes.

Fiserv

DefinitionThe processing backbone that Fractal — and both the Sky and Fractal product lines — ultimately run on.

Why a rep should careIt's the shared foundation under both divisions. You won't sell it, but you should know the ground you're standing on.

For exampleBoth Sky merchant services and Command Cloud / EasyPay share the Fiserv processing backbone.

Who to callChris & Misty, in Fractal Ops.

Gateway

DefinitionThe secure technology that carries payment data between SkyOS and the processor, encrypting it in transit.

Why a rep should careIt's the plumbing behind "is this secure?" — data is encrypted and never stored in the clear.

For exampleThe gateway encrypts each transaction before it's submitted for authorization.

Payment rails

DefinitionThe underlying networks that move money — card networks, ACH, RTP, wire.

Why a rep should care"Pay by Bank rides ACH, not Visa" is a rail distinction — and it's the whole reason it's cheaper.

For examplePay by Bank uses the ACH rail instead of the card networks.

Required disclosure — memorize thisWhenever you discuss SkyFi banking: "SkyFi is not a bank; banking services are provided by Grasshopper Bank, N.A., Member FDIC. FDIC insurance only covers the failure of the bank itself." This is not optional phrasing — it must appear in SkyFi-related materials and conversations.

The people on the other end of the phone

Who we actually sell to

Everything above is our world. This is theirs. Our ideal customers cluster into three worlds that look alike from the outside but pay — and get paid — in completely different ways. Learn how each runs, the words they use, how money flows, and the software you'll walk into. Each software note defines the kind of tool first — the durable knowledge — then names real examples.

first world · you're talking to the merchant

Material producers

They make or supply the physical stuff a jobsite runs on — ready-mix concrete, aggregates, asphalt, lumber, building supply. They sell a product and collect for it, which makes them the merchant. This is our sweet spot, because collecting money is exactly where card fees, fraud, and slow AR live.

How they work

An order comes in, product is produced or loaded, and it's delivered with a ticket that proves quantity. Then one of two things happens: the customer pays at delivery (COD), or they're billed to pay later (on account). That single flag decides everything downstream.

How money flows

COD means cash, check, or card at or before delivery — the driver, dispatcher, or office can take it. Account means an invoice on net terms and no money at the jobsite. Card is common and rising, which is exactly where the fee pain bites.

The software you'll walk into
Dispatching software — the operational hub.
The system that runs their whole day: it takes the order, schedules and tracks the truck or load, and generates the delivery ticket. If a producer names one of these, that's where their operation lives — and often where invoices originate.
e.g. Command Alkon / Command Cloud, Sysdyne, Keystone.
Batching software — plant production control.
Controls the actual mixing and production at the plant — proportioning materials to spec. Usually tied into dispatch rather than a separate tool the office logs into.
e.g. Command Alkon (Integra / Apex), Sysdyne. · verify
ERP — enterprise resource planning.
The big system that runs the whole business — books, inventory, orders, operations — in one place. Larger producers run their invoicing and AR from here.
e.g. Epicor BisTrack, DMSi Agility, ECI Spruce, Acumatica, NetSuite.
Accounting software — the books & invoicing.
Narrower than an ERP — it tracks the books, invoicing, and AR/AP but doesn't run operations. Smaller producers bill straight from here. The key discovery question: where does the card or ACH actually get captured — inside this, or a processor bolted on beside it?
e.g. QuickBooks, Sage.
Their words

COD vs. account customer · ticket · net 30 / 60 / 90 · will-call (customer picks up rather than gets delivery) · DSO / AR aging · MCC. If these come up naturally, you're talking to a material producer.

second world · they buy material and build

Contractors

They buy material from the producers above and perform the build — a house, a parking lot, a commercial site. Their world is project-based: they win a job, run it through phases, and get paid as they make progress. They think in projects and schedules, not tickets and loads.

How they work

They win a job (bid or referral), then run it from planning through to completion. Because work spans weeks or months, they bill their customer in progress and pay their own suppliers on account. This is where the solutioning timeline vocabulary — planning, design, development, testing, live — actually shows up in conversation.

How money flows

They're often paid in stages as the project advances, and they carry the cost of labor and materials in between. Cash timing — not backlog — is what caps how much work they can run at once.

The software you'll walk into
Project management software — the go-to for this world.
The system that plans, schedules, tracks, and documents a construction project from bid to closeout — budgets, change orders, daily logs, and often billing all in one place. If a contractor names one of these, it's the spine of how they run jobs.
e.g. Procore, Buildertrend, CoConstruct.
Accounting / ERP — the books behind the jobs.
Underneath the project tool, the books still live somewhere — construction-flavored accounting or a general system, tracking job costing and AP/AR.
e.g. QuickBooks, Sage 100/300 Contractor, Foundation.
Their words

bid / job · progress billing · change order · closeout · net terms to their suppliers · the project lifecycle language. They talk about projects and phases, not deliveries.

third world · you're talking to the payee

Heavy civil

A specialized contractor: they build public infrastructure — roads, bridges, water systems — for public owners like a state DOT or a city. Here the position flips: nobody collects at a point of sale. They perform work and get billed to a large public owner. They're the payee, not the merchant — which is exactly why card usually has no place in their world.

How they work

Work is won, not ordered — a competitive bid to a public owner, or a subcontract under a prime. There's no single delivery; progress is measured in quantities against the contract's line items.

How money flows

Monthly pay applications (a DOT calls it a progress estimate) get submitted, the owner's engineer certifies the quantities, and payment comes about net 30 by ACH or wire — minus retainage (5–10% held back until the whole job closes). No card, anywhere. You can't surcharge a government owner.

The software you'll walk into
The owner's payment portal — they're a guest in it.
Unlike the other worlds, heavy civil firms don't get paid through their own software — they submit into the customer's portal, a different one per job. That fragmentation is a real pain point, but it's not a card problem.
e.g. WAWF / PIEE (DoD), IPP (federal), AASHTOWare Project (state DOT), Textura, GCPay, Procore Pay.
Their words

pay app · progress estimate · retainage · the owner · the prime · sub · lien waiver · certified payroll. If you hear these, you're in heavy civil — qualify hard before spending cycles.

the one qualifying signal

It's not sector or size — it's card

Across all three worlds, the fastest read on whether there's a real opportunity is a single question: does this business accept card payments today, and do they want to? Card is a live, growing surface for material producers, and essentially absent for heavy civil. If cards aren't part of how they get paid and there's no appetite for them, the fit is thin no matter how big the logo.

Sounds like a fit

"COD" and "on account" come up naturally · customers are contractors, builders, homeowners · they mention dispatch, tickets, drivers · they already take some card and the fees sting · they set their own terms.

Qualify hard

"Pay apps," "retainage," "the owner," "the prime" · customers are DOTs and agencies · they log into someone else's portal to get paid · everything is ACH or wire and card never comes up · terms are dictated to them.
